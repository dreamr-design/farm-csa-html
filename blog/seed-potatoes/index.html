Farm notes · From the rows at Bramblecut

Forty rows of seed potatoes, and why we wait for the oaks

Potatoes go in when the oak leaves are the size of a squirrel's ear. How we cut and cure 1,800 pounds of seed, and the old tractor that plants them.

Written after choresMay 6, 2026

WrittenWed.packed Thurs.

Every farmer in Vernon County has a rule for when potatoes go in. My father-in-law's was Good Friday. Walt, who rented us our first acre, said you plant potatoes when the oak leaves are the size of a squirrel's ear. Walt was right more often, so we wait for the oaks. This year they got there on Monday, and by Saturday forty rows of seed potatoes will be in the ground on the east slope.

Cutting the seed

A seed potato is just a potato. You plant a piece of last year's crop and it grows a plant that makes ten more. We bring in 1,800 pounds of certified organic seed each spring, in four varieties:

  • Red Norland, early and waxy, the first new potatoes in your July crates;
  • Yukon Gold, the all-rounder;
  • Kennebec, a white baker that keeps until March;
  • Adirondack Blue, which stays blue when you cook it and makes children eat potatoes.

Anything bigger than an egg gets cut so every piece has two or three eyes. Then the pieces sit on screens in the barn for three days so the cut sides dry and form a skin. A cured piece does not rot in cold soil. An uncured one does.

The planter

Our potato planter is older than everyone on the crew put together. It is a two-row hopper from 1951 that Tomás found in a fencerow in 2016, pulled behind the 1958 tractor. Two people ride on the back and drop a piece into each cup as the chain goes round. It is slow and loud, and it plants an acre a day.

The tractor needs a push to start, the planter needs a hammer to adjust, and between them they have never missed a spring.

Hilling, and waiting

Three weeks after planting, the first leaves break the soil and we start hilling: pulling soil up over the stems so the new potatoes form in the dark. Potatoes that see light turn green and bitter. We hill twice, in June and early July, and in between we walk the rows looking for the striped beetles that love potato leaves as much as we do. We pick them by hand into a can of soapy water.

The first new potatoes come out in the second week of July. Thin skins, no peeling, boiled and buttered. If you want them in your crate, there are still Half and Full shares for the season that starts June 10.

Tomás Rivera, co-owner, soil and machines
