Twelve heirlooms, one long table: notes from the Tomato Supper

Forty guests, five courses and one very wet tablecloth. How we picked the twelve varieties, and the two going into every bed next year.

Written after choresAugust 26, 2026

WrittenWed.packed Thurs.

The Tomato Supper sold out in four days this year, which tells me two things. People want to eat tomatoes in a field, and I should have set more seats. On Saturday, August 22, forty of you sat at one long table between the squash rows, and Dev cooked five courses around a single fruit. Here is how it went.

Choosing twelve

We grow thirty-one kinds of tomato. Choosing twelve for one dinner is the hardest planning I do all year, and I plan the whole farm. In January I make a list of every variety that ripens in the third week of August, then Dev and I taste last year's notes out loud and argue. These made the table:

  • Cherokee Purple and Paul Robeson, the two dark ones, smoky and rich;
  • Green Zebra, sharp enough to wake up a plate;
  • Sungold, the cherry that never makes it back to the barn;
  • Brandywine, Pineapple and Striped German, the three big slicers;
  • Black Krim, Amish Paste, Jaune Flammée, Speckled Roman and Wapsipinicon Peach.

Five courses

Dev started with tomato water, the clear juice of twelve varieties strained overnight, served cold in jam jars. Then a salad of every slicer, cut thick, with nothing but salt and our own oil. Then the Green Zebra gazpacho, the roasted Amish Paste over polenta, and for dessert a Sungold sorbet that three guests asked for the recipe to. He will not give it out.

A tomato in August needs a knife, salt and a table. Everything else is a cook trying to show off, and I was showing off on purpose.

The wet tablecloth

At 8:40, just as the sorbet went out, a cloud we had all ignored opened over the table. Forty people picked up their bowls and walked into the high tunnel, where we finished dessert standing among the very plants we had been eating. Nobody left. The tablecloth is still drying in the barn.

Two for every bed

We asked every guest to vote for one variety on a crate tag. Sungold won, as it always does. The surprise was Wapsipinicon Peach, a small fuzzy yellow tomato from Iowa that took eleven votes. Both go into every tomato bed next year, so they will be in your crates by August.

The Harvest Supper on October 10 still has a few seats. Write to the farm to hold one.

Mae Lindqvist, co-owner and grower
